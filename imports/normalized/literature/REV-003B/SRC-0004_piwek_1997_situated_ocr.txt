                                                Abstract
      In this paper, a formal model of communication in dialogues is described. Recently, a shift
      seems to have taken place from the traditional perception of the computer as a mere tool for
      carrying out a task towards a view of the computer as an assistant with which one or more
      users work together — i.e., cooperate — on a task. Now, cooperation is rooted in coordination
      of actions, which in turn can be achieved through communication. The model for cooperative
      behaviour in dialogues that we propose is based on observational studies into human-human
      communication carried out at IPO and findings from Discourse Analysis and Conversation
      Analysis. Our main concern will be to explain the role of the basic constituents of conversa-
      tion that are known as adjacency pairs. Central in our model are rules that describe how the
      commitments of the dialogue partners are updated during the course of a dialogue and how
      they constrain the possible moves of the dialogue participants. We take communication to be
      part of the overall activity in which the interlocutors are engaged. Such a model is needed if
      we want to account for the fact that information is often exchanged in dialogues in a sequence
      of alternating (combinations of) modalities (linguistic means, object manipulations and/or
      gestures). The model was used for the behaviour rules of an artificial assistant that is imple-
      mented as part of the DenK (Dialogue Modelling and Knowledge Acquisition) project.



Introduction
In the past decade, there has been a trend towards building systems that give users the idea
that they are working together with an assistant on a task rather than doing the work on
their own. Building such systems requires an understanding of what makes behaviour co-
operative. Grice (1975) proposed that in conversations cooperative behaviour partly de-
pends on the fact that the interlocutors obey the maxim be relevant. In this paper, we
present a partial analysis of this maxim. This analysis includes the description of a com-
putational model of a dialogue agent that can produce relevant actions.
       The type of relevance to which we limit our attention has been introduced by con-
versation analysts under the name conditional relevance. On the basis of large corpora of
naturally occurring dialogues, conversation analysts came to the conclusion that the basic
building blocks of conversations are utterance pairs, often referred to as adjacency pairs.!
The first and the second part of such a pair are tied together by relevance: ‘When one ut-
terance (A) is conditionally relevant on another (S), then the occurrence of S provides for
the relevance of the occurrence of A. If A occurs, it occurs (i.e., is produced and heard)
as “responsive to” S, ...” (Schegloff, 1972, p. 76). For instance, an answer is relevant for
the question that the speaker intends to answer.
      Now, we can specify our goal more precisely. We aim at providing a model, given
an utterance u and the context c of this utterance, that computes a set of responses R to u,

such that every response r in R is conditionally relevant for u. The model is supposed to
clarify the informal notion of conditional relevance. Note that we assume the second part
is dependent on the first part and the context. Furthermore, the model does not yield a
unique second part for a context and a first part. This is due to the fact that some aspects
of the context are not captured by our model. More specifically, we do not work out how
an addressee chooses to believe one piece of information rather than another piece of in-
formation that is inconsistent with the aforementioned information.
      Our model is based on the conception that actions, whether directly on the physical
world or via the use of symbols, are only effective in virtue offeedback. We base this view
on a theory of action (Perceptual Control Theory) proposed in Powers (1972). The other
ingredient of our model is a formal notion of context and context change. For this part, we
extend techniques developed in formal semantics (e.g., Stalnaker, 1978; Kamp, 1981).
       The task we have set ourselves might seem to lack ambition. The danger is of ending
up with a model that states such trivia as: an agent can react to a question with an answer
and to a command with a signal of compliance. Besides the fact that it is quite difficult to
spell out in detail what counts as an answer, one challenging problem is that, for instance,
questions do not always require an answer (only). Take, ‘Can you pass me the salt?’.
Though this sentence expresses a question, the addressee is usually expected to do more
than just answer it. Moreover, the relation between the parts of the following exchange is
not trivial: ‘A: It is getting late, Mildred. B: But I’m having such fun’ (based on Levinson,
1983, p. 290). Our model covers these cases.
      The remainder of this paper consists of the following parts. In the next section, we
briefly describe the conceptions of action and context, which we use as a point of depar-
ture for our model. Subsequently, we present the model itself and analyse some dialogue
fragments using the model. Finally, we present some concluding remarks.


Action, reaction and context
The importance of feedback for action is the main tenet of Perceptual Control Theory
(PCT; Powers, 1972). According to PCT behaviour is the control of perception. Roughly
speaking, an agent has an internal reference signal (the technical term for a goal) and at-
tempts to minimize the difference between this reference signal and its perceptions of the
world through action.
       When an agent acts in the physical world, e.g., when trying to open a door, the feed-
back is often instantaneous. Note that this is very useful if an agent has several goals that
stand in relations of dependency. Suppose our agent wants to enter a room after having
opened the door to the room. Clearly, the agent can only initiate the appropriate action af-
ter it has observed that the door is open (as a consequence of opening the door). As long
as this information is not yet available, the agent cannot initiate the act of entering the
room, since its success depends on the success of the door opening action. Alternatively,
if the agent receives the feedback that the door is locked, it can also undertake the appro-
priate actions (e.g., fetch the keys). In other words, without feedback the agent is left with-
out knowing what to do next.
       In naturally occurring dialogues, a human agent often acts via another human agent.
For instance, an agent might issue a command which the addressee then carries out. The
action does not need to be performed immediately and it may not be perceptible to the is-

  suer of the command, like for example a telephone conversation. This clearly creates a
  feedback problem. We propose to interpret the adjacency pairs that have been observed
  by conversation analysts as supplying the natural solution to this problem.
       Basically, the second part of an adjacency pair supplies information about what the
  reaction is or will be to the first part. Note that nevertheless there is still a significant dif-
  ference between physical actions and actions in a conversation. A physical action directly
  causes a change in the physical world. If the physical world 1s changed by issuing a com-
  mand, a more intricate situation arises. An agent can, for instance, tell another agent the
  following “Hand in your paper first thing in the morning’. In this case, the speaker at-
  tempts to bring about an action through changing the disposition of the addressee to act
  as required. If the addressee accepts the command, then he/she will become committed
  and, therefore, disposed to carry it out. Note that this requires storage of commitments.
  The general issue of information storage as part of communication has been examined ex-
  tensively in formal semantics. These examinations gave rise to several frameworks for
. modelling information states and the dynamics of these states.
        In formal semantics, the insight that the meaning of sentences resides in context-
  change effects has become widely acknowledged in the past fifteen years (mainly due to
  problems concerning cross-sentential anaphora, e.g., Kamp, 1981). Many frameworks
  have been proposed to model context change. Two components are common to all these
  frameworks.
       Firstly, there is some notion of the information state. The information state covers
 part of the context. It models the dialogue situation as it is perceived by the agent. It does
 not include aspects of the context that are external to the agent. Secondly, the meaning of
 an utterance is specified as a transition between information states. An information state
 can, for instance, be modelled as a collection of sentences in a formal language (these sen-
 tences represent the explicit beliefs of the agent to whom the information state belongs;
 the implicit beliefs consist of the logical consequences of the explicit beliefs). Consider
 an utterance of the sentence ‘Mary walks’. The effect is that the information state before
 the utterance is extended with the translation (into a formal language) of “Mary walks’.
 The advantage of identifying the information state with a set of sentences belonging to a
 formal language is that it allows us to mechanically derive the logical consequences of
 these sentences.
       This aforementioned type of framework is well-suited for modelling monologues,
 where one can focus on the effect that a monologue has on the information state of the
 addressee. When it comes to dialogue, there are some evident limitations. It is not clear
 what to do with requests and questions. Furthermore, even assertions do not fit the
 straightforward information state change model: an agent does not automatically update
 his or her information state when confronted with new information; more accurately, it
 evaluates the information in the light of the information that is already available to it.
 What is more, we have assumed that information is always transmitted without any diffi-
 culty. This is not a realistic assumption. In fact, dialogues often contain exchanges that
 are meant to overcome transmission problems. Such exchanges do not fit the aforemen-
 tioned framework either. Finally, we need the means to represent which information ‘be-
 longs’ to which agent. In other words, which agent is committed to which information.
 Each of these issues will be addressed in the next section.

Context change in dialogues
In this section, we present our notion of an information state for dialogues — henceforth,
the dialogue state — and some rules governing the change of such states. A dialogue state
consists of two main parts: firstly, a part that records which interlocutor is committed to
which information, and secondly, a part that contains representations of utterances about
which the interlocutors still have to reach some form of agreement. It is important to re-
member that an information state is always associated with an agent. In other words, it is
a subjective view on the dialogue situation. There is no dialogue state independent of the
representation that each of the interlocutors has of it. In the following, we model the in-
formation state of agent A and assume that A is engaged in a dialogue with agent B.

Commitments
Agent A’s dialogue state records which information A and B are each committed to. Com-
mitments constrain both the linguistic and non-linguistic actions of an agent by requiring
the actions to be compatible with the content of the commitments. In case of linguistic ac-
tions, this means that an agent is not expected/supposed to assert a proposition if it is com-
mitted to its denial. The counterpart of this constraint is proposed in Hamblin (1971). He
points out that an agent should agree to a statement if it is committed to this statement or
some statement from which it clearly follows. There also are systematic relations between
commitments and the physical actions of an agent. If an agent is committed to the infor-
mation that some event will occur, it is not expected to undertake actions that prevent this
event from happening. In case the event concerns an action by the agent itself, it is expect-
ed to carry out the action. In other words, commitment of an agent to a (future) action is
equivalent to the agent intending to do that action.”
      During a dialogue, the interlocutors maintain a record of their own and each other’s
commitments. Formally, the commitments A ascribes to itself and B are sets of sentences
(in a formal language). Henceforth we shall refer to these sets as 4 and I’p. Some sen-
tences may be a member of both I’, and Ip. This means that there are some commitments
which A believes A and B both have.

      We already pointed out that an agent can reason with the sentences (which represent
commitments) in its information state. We will assume that there are commitments which
 play a specific role in reasoning; they are called default rules. The prototypical default
 rule corresponds to ‘If something is a bird, then it can fly’. Suppose we obtain the infor-
 mation that ‘Tweety is a bird’. Then we may derive that ‘Tweety can fly’, using the afore-
 mentioned rule. Thus commitment to the default rule and to the information that “Tweety
‘is a bird’ commits us to ‘Tweety can fly’. If, however, we now come to know that ‘Tweety
 is a penguin’, and we are committed to the information that penguins are exceptions to the
 aforementioned rule (‘If something is a penguin, then it cannot fly’), then this new infor-
 mation blocks inferring the commitment that ‘Tweety can fly’. In other words, if one is
committed to P due to a default rule, then this commitment is cancelled if one becomes
committed to some piece of information that contradicts P.

Change
We are now going to look into the immediate change of the information state effected by
an utterance. For that purpose, we look into the second part of the information state, which
consists of two components: A; and Ap. Both are modelled as lists. These components are
used to (temporarily) store, the informative and the dialectical dimension of an utterance,
respectively. This is best explained by examining what effect utterances of the basic three
sentence types have on A, and Ap.
      Take an utterance of the declarative sentence ‘It rains’. We call this a declarative
act. Both interlocutors will add a representation to their A, of (i) the content of the sen-
tence (this is a judgment, i.e., a statement of how the world is, has been or will be), which
in this case is simply a formal equivalent of ‘It rains’, and (ii) the information that the
speaker is publicly committed to this judgment. For instance, if A uttered ‘It rains’, then
A expressed its own commitment to ‘It rains’. The dialectical move made by uttering this
sentence is that the judgment ‘It rains’ is now under discussion, i.e., it is added to Ap. In
other words, A invites B to present its position — acceptance or denial — with respect to
this judgment.
       The utterance of the imperative sentence ‘Open the door’, an imperative act or re- |
quest, consists of addition to A, of (i) the judgment that the addressee opens the door, and
(11) the information that (i) is a commitment of the addressee. In other words, the speaker
imposes a commitment on the addressee. The move has no dialectical dimension; it does
not affect Ap. An utterance of an interrogative sentence ‘Does it rain?’ (an interrogative


                                                93
act) can be seen as a specialized type of imperative act. It consists of (1) the judgment that
the addressee answers the question ‘Does it rain?’, and (ii) the information that (i) is a
commitment of the addressee.

Some applications
Consider the following exchange, which is based on Levinson (1983, p. 290), where it is
introduced as a problem for speech act theory.*

        A: It is getting late, Mildred.
        B: But, I’m having fun.

Now suppose that I’, p contains the default rule ‘If it is getting late and X is at a party, then
X goes home’. Furthermore, suppose B accepts that it is getting late, but is having fun and
therefore does not want to be committed to going home. B has the following dilemma: if
B accepts A’s assertion, B also accepts that they go home. B’s utterance has been devised
to cancel this commitment. In other words, B reacts to the judgment ‘It is getting late’ by
cancelling one of its default implications, assuming that ‘if X is having fun, X normally
does not go home’. By taking I", into account we can model that B’s utterance is condi-
tionally relevant for that of A. This suggests the following addressee-based strategy: when
accepting a judgment, check whether acceptance leads to undesired commitments. If pos-
sible, cancel the commitments in question explicitly. So-called indirect speech acts can be
treated along similar lines. Consider:

      A: Can you pass me the salt.
      B: Sure, here you are.

Suppose we have the following default cooperation rule as a part of I", p: if an interlocutor
X can do action o then X does a. This means that answering the question with ‘Sure’ at
the same time commits B by default to B passes the salt. That B indeed was committed to
B passes the salt is witnessed by saying ‘here you are’. Note that for the case B wants to
answer ‘yes’ but does not want to carry out the action, the agent should use ‘but’: compare
‘Sure, but I won’t’ with ‘Sure, I won’t’. The latter construction seems extremely odd. The
obligatory use of ‘but’ is evidence for the fact that we are dealing with default inferences
(‘but’ is usually assigned the meaning of contrast or presentation of non-expected infor-
mation. Exceptions to a default are typical pieces of non-expected information).
      Finally, we want to demonstrate the value of our model in a multimodal setting.
Consider the following exchange, which is a translation of a Dutch exchange from
Cremers (1993, p. 9). The setting for this exchange was as follows: A and B can commu-
nicate through typed input via terminals and share a visually perceptible workspace, in
which B can manipulate blocks and A may only point at those blocks:

      A: Remove the uppermost of the two green cubes.
      B: (does it).
      A: OK...

After A’s first utterance, B becomes committed to removing the uppermost of the two
green cubes. B signals this by performing the action. This action, however, also has a di-
alectical dimension: the judgment under discussion is ‘that B removed the uppermost of
the two green cubes’. Because A is the expert in this setting and it is crucial that the right
blocks are moved, A explicitly accepts the judgment that was under discussion. Thus, it
becomes a shared commitment that B took the right block.


Concluding remarks
In this paper, we explained the notion of conditional relevance in terms of a model of in-
formation state change in dialogue. The crucial ingredients of the model are a broad no-
tion of commitments (covering what traditionally have been called beliefs and intentions)


                                              95
and the storing of information whose status has not yet been settled. The conversational
rules we proposed operated on the information that has not yet been settled, but always
against the background of the (shared) commitments. Furthermore, we analysed requests
in terms of commitments that a speaker assigns to a hearer. We showed how the presence
of default rules amongst the shared commitments can account for a certain amount of in-
directness in conversations. The default rules were proposed as being used by the dialogue
agents. In this respect, our application of defaults differs from that in Perrault (1990) and
Beun (1994), where default rules are used to reason about the behaviour of the dialogue
agents from a third person perspective.
      It was beyond the scope of this paper to present a detailed comparison with alterna-
tive proposals from the literature. For that purpose, we refer to Piwek (1998) and Piwek
(to appear), where we compare our approach with traditional speech act theory and sug-
gest how our model contributes to modern collaborative planning theories (e.g., Loch-
baum, 1994) by providing a detailed analysis of the relation between the linguistic form
and the function of utterances.
      Finally, we would like to point out that substantial parts of the information state
change model for dialogue that we have described have been used in the DenK system
(see Ahn, Beun, Borghuis, Bunt & Van Overveld, 1995; Bunt, Ahn, Kievit, Piwek, Ver-
linden, Beun & Borghuis, 1998).
